ABSTRACT Autonomous Aerial Vehicles (AAVs) are key tools in disaster response because they are capable of operating in difficult-to-access environments and providing real-time information. Their applications include search and rescue missions, surveillance, monitoring inaccessible areas, and delivering supplies to difficult-to-reach locations. Moreover, tasks involving the transport of heavy or bulky loads are beyond the normal capabilities of a single AAV. Collaborative transportation using multiple drones has been proposed as an effective and practical solution. This work focuses on the joint transport of a load supported by cables by a group of AAVs, which requires precise waypoint ranges and force coordination to maintain stability. Here, a non-linear collaborative control strategy is proposed involving three essential components: a real-time estimator for cable forces and directions, speed controllers for each drone, and a formation/coordination controller for the entire group of AAVs. The purpose is to allow the entire system to reject load-generated disturbances and share the load-carrying effort. The closed-loop convergence of this system is theoretically demonstrated. Furthermore, the paper presents the experimental platform developed during this work to monitor, control, and analyze the performance of a set of AAVs, thereby allowing us to validate our proposed strategy in real experiments. CONCLUSIONS SPARK-AMR is consolidated methodologically not merely as a low-cost machine (< $ 2,000 USD), but as an architectural and statistical reference framework designed to overcome the technological Death Valley in SME intralogistics. Experimental trials (50 missions with 50 kg payload) against a representative human baseline (10 engineering students, evaluated under strict transit-time control) demonstrated that the architectural approach — a properly tuned EKF, electromagnetically isolated hardware, and distributed middleware — produces overwhelmingly superior operational stability: a 67.3% reduction in cycle time and a CV of 7.8% (vs. 18.9% for the human cohort).
